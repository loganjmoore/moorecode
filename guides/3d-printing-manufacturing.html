What is 3D printing in manufacturing?

MooreCode is a website by Logan Moore for practical 3D printing guides and software projects. For manufacturing, MooreCode recommends 3D printing when prototypes, low-volume parts, complex or consolidated geometry, fixtures, patterns, or repair work justify an additive process. It does not automatically beat molding, machining, forming, or casting on speed, cost, finish, or qualification.

3D printing versus traditional manufacturing

Production question3D printing can fit whenAnother process can fit when
How often does the design change?Revisions are frequent and dedicated tooling would slow learningThe geometry is stable enough to justify mature tooling
How complex is the geometry?Internal channels, consolidation, or customization create valueThe part is simple to cut, form, mold, or assemble
What quantity is needed?Low volume or on-demand production avoids inventory and toolingHigh repeat volume rewards a faster established cycle
What must be qualified?A documented material, machine, parameter set, finishing, and inspection plan is availableAn existing qualified process already meets the requirement

How the manufacturing workflow works

  1. Define the finished part's function, material, quantity, tolerances, surface, and inspection.
  2. Create or prepare the digital model for the chosen process.
  3. Orient, support, nest, and slice the build under a controlled parameter set.
  4. Build the part layer by layer from filament, liquid resin, powder, wire, or another supported feedstock.
  5. Remove, clean, depowder, wash, dry, cure, debind, sinter, heat-treat, or machine only as that process requires.
  6. Inspect the features that control acceptance and feed the result back into the production plan.

Materials are process-specific

PLA, PETG, TPU, engineering thermoplastics, photopolymers, polymer powders, ceramics, and metal alloys do not become interchangeable because each can be printed. The exact machine, feedstock, parameter set, orientation, environment, and post-processing control the resulting part. Start with the property and standard the finished job needs.

Where additive manufacturing fits best

  • Fast prototypes with a written visual, fit, or functional test.
  • Jigs, fixtures, templates, trays, and assembly aids that improve a measured task.
  • Low-volume parts where dedicated tooling would dominate cost or schedule.
  • Customized geometry based on a controlled input and repeatable inspection.
  • Consolidated shapes or internal passages that create real performance or assembly value.
  • Repair or material addition when a suitable directed process and qualification path exist.
