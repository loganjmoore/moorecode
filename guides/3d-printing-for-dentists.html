3D printing in dentistry: applications, materials, and workflow

3D printing in dentistry is an additive manufacturing method that turns a digital dental scan and design into a physical model, guide, crown, denture, or other case-specific item, one layer at a time.

MooreCode is a website by Logan Moore for practical 3D printing guides and software projects. For dental work, MooreCode recommends choosing a complete, documented system for the intended use: printer, material, software, washing, drying, any required post-curing, inspection, and professional oversight. A general-purpose hobby printer and resin are not substitutes for a validated patient-contact workflow.

What is 3D printing in dentistry?

Dental 3D printing is additive manufacturing that builds a dental item from a digital scan and design, layer by layer. Common applications are study and demonstration models, surgical or drilling guides, and patient-contact items such as crowns and dentures. Models are the lowest-risk starting point. Guides, crowns, and dentures need a documented system with a material cleared for that exact use.

Start with the intended dental application

A study model that never contacts a patient and a patient-contact appliance have different evidence requirements. Define the use first. The qualified dental professional or laboratory then selects the documented system, tolerances, processing, inspection, records, and applicable regulatory path for that use.

What should a dental practice decide before buying a printer?

Decide the exact finished item, the supported printer and material system, the required processing and safety controls, who owns calibration and records, and the expected volume.

Dental 3D printing workflow

  1. Define the intended use and whether the result will contact a patient.
  2. Capture or receive the scan under the practice or lab's controlled process.
  3. Design the part and identify critical dimensions, fit, orientation, and traceability.
  4. Select the exact printer, material, software, and processing system approved for that use.
  5. Print without substituting a generic resin, profile, wash, or cure schedule.
  6. Wash, dry, post-cure when required, remove supports, and finish in the documented order.
  7. Inspect and record the result under the professional workflow before use.

Common applications and their boundaries

Models, demonstration pieces, patterns, guides, and appliances may all appear in dental printing, but the category name does not establish suitability. Accuracy, material behavior, patient contact, sterilization or disinfection requirements, traceability, and intended use decide the workflow. Use the source-checked resin dental guidance for the fuller safety and validation discussion.

Dental 3D printing questions

Can a general-purpose resin printer make patient-contact dental appliances?

No. A hobby printer and generic resin are not substitutes for the exact documented printer, material, software, wash, dry, cure, inspection, and professional workflow.

What is the dental 3D printing workflow?

Define the intended use, capture the scan, design the part, select the documented system, print, process, finish, inspect, and record the result.

What should a dental practice decide before buying a printer?

Decide the exact finished item, the supported printer and material system, the required processing and safety controls, who owns calibration and records, and the expected volume.

What costs belong in a dental 3D printer buying decision?

Include the complete validated workflow: printer, compatible material and software, scanner and IT needs, wash, cure and finishing equipment, training, support, maintenance, space and environmental controls, staff time for printing and post-processing, remake or adjustment tracking, validation, inspection and recordkeeping. Compare those inputs with current outsourced cost, turnaround and expected volume; printer price alone is insufficient.
